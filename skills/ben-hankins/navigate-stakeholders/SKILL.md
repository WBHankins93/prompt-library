**When to use this skill:** a technically credible solution still depends on several people saying
yes — or one overlooked person choosing not to — and you need to navigate the decision as a system.

**What it produces:** an influence map, audience-specific messages, uncovered relationships and
objections, and a sequenced plan for building a coalition that can carry the decision.

## Map power, pain, and participation separately

Do not confuse the org chart with the decision. For each stakeholder, identify three things:

- **Power** — what can they approve, block, delay, or redefine?
- **Pain** — what outcome, risk, or workload makes the initiative matter to them?
- **Participation** — will they evaluate, buy, implement, operate, govern, or merely influence?

The executive sponsor may own the budget but rely on an architect for technical permission and an
operations lead for practical consent. Record evidence for each judgment. A title is a clue, not
proof of influence.

## Find the missing veto

Look for people who inherit risk without receiving visible upside: security, operations,
procurement, compliance, and adjacent platform owners. They are often invited late and asked to
approve a decision they did not help shape. That creates a rational veto.

Treat repeated phrases such as "we still need to check with…" as signals of hidden authority. Ask
who must live with the result, who has stopped similar work before, and whose absence would make a
decision reversible. Bring likely veto holders in early enough to change the plan, not just bless it.

## Give each audience a truthful reason to support the same outcome

Keep one shared decision, but change the emphasis:

- Technical evaluators need precision, constraints, and honest product boundaries.
- Operators need evidence that the result is supportable under failure and routine change.
- Business owners need the outcome, timing, cost of inaction, and material risks.
- Governance stakeholders need control, accountability, and a credible exception path.

Translate without hiding complexity. If two audiences require incompatible outcomes, surface the
conflict as a decision; do not paper it over with different stories.

## Build a coalition through earned commitments

Move from passive interest to specific commitments: review the architecture, provide data, define a
success measure, approve a control, or sponsor the next meeting. A stakeholder who agrees in a call
but owns no next action is not yet part of the coalition.

Use the champion to learn the landscape, not as a courier for every difficult conversation. Equip
them with language and evidence they can reuse, while preserving direct access to the people whose
judgment can change the outcome.

## Re-map after every material change

Scope, timing, cost, and newly discovered risk redistribute influence. Revisit the map when any of
them changes. Track who gained exposure, whose concern remains unanswered, and whether the current
meeting sequence matches the real decision sequence. Silence after a material change is not consent;
it is an unresolved signal.

## What good looks like

The decision has no surprise approver, late-stage veto, or audience hearing a contradictory story.
Each stakeholder can explain why the initiative matters in language natural to their role, and the
people inheriting the operational risk helped shape the plan. Support survives when the original
champion leaves the room because the coalition is wider than one relationship.

## Hard rules

- NEVER equate seniority with influence; verify who the room follows when tradeoffs appear.
- NEVER route every hard question through the champion; dependency on one person is deal fragility.
- NEVER give different audiences incompatible versions of the truth.
- NEVER treat silence, attendance, or verbal enthusiasm as a commitment.
- ALWAYS involve the people who inherit risk before asking them to approve it.
